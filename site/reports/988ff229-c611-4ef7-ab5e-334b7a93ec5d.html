Railway Convention Committee's Ninth Report Examines Cost of Operations, Staffing, and Fuel Expenses

Report date: 1983-08-26

Committee: Railway Convention Committee

Lok Sabha: 07

What was discussed

The Railway Convention Committee (1980) presented its Ninth Report on the 'Cost of Operation of Railways (Staff & Fuel Cost)'. The committee examined the overall costs of operating the Indian Railways, with a specific focus on the expenses related to staff and fuel. This examination aimed to identify areas for potential economies, allowing the railways to increase appropriations to various funds, pay dividends to General Revenues, and improve service quality.

The committee gathered information and took evidence from representatives of the Ministry of Railways on June 2 and November 22, 1982, and June 24 and 25, 1983. It also received memoranda and evidence from former officials, including Shri B.S.D. Baliga (Retd. Chairman, Railway Board), Shri P.N. Kaul (Retd. Member (Mechanical), Railway Board), and Shri R.K. Nalesan (Retd. General Manager, Northern Railway), as well as other individuals.

The report specifically reviewed the operating ratio, which is a measure of an organization's working results in financial terms, defined as the percentage of total working expenses to total earnings. It also delved into working expenses, which encompass administration, operation, maintenance, repairs, and appropriations to the Pension Fund and Depreciation Reserve Fund. The committee's succeeding chapters covered its examination of staff and fuel costs.

What the report found

The report found that the operating ratio of the Railways fluctuated, rising from 81.00% in 1950-51 to 96.01% in 1980-81, before reducing to 89.40% in 1981-82. This reduction was attributed to a record increase in goods loading, higher earnings from fares and freight, and strict expenditure control. However, in 1981-82, the Eastern, North Eastern, Northeast Frontier, and Southern Railways still had operating ratios exceeding 100%, indicating losses, with the North Eastern Railway's ratio deteriorating further from 134.5% in 1980-81 to 155.8% in 1981-82.

Working expenses (excluding dividend payments) increased nearly four times from Rs. 862.22 crores in 1970-71 to Rs. 3,224.70 crores in 1981-82. This increase was primarily due to escalating input costs. From 1970-71 to 1981-82, the average annual cost per employee increased by 172.7%, fuel cost by 179.6% to 332.1%, and other stores by 169.5% to 331.6%. In contrast, average fare rates increased by 79.2% and average freight rates by 152.3%. In 1981-82, staff costs accounted for 45.8% of total ordinary working expenses, fuel and electricity for traction 24.6%, and other materials 29.6%.

The Indian Railways employed over 15.7 lakh regular employees and nearly 2.3 lakh casual labourers, with the wage bill for regular employees being Rs. 1451 crores in 1981-82. The average annual cost per employee increased by 634% from Rs. 1,263 in 1950-51 to Rs. 9,267 in 1981-82, while the cost of living index rose by 443% during the same period. Staff strength increased by 14.4% between 1970-71 and 1980-81, with higher increases in Groups A, B, and C categories partly due to reclassification of doctors, creation of the Railway Protection Force, and upgrading of posts, as well as the need for more skilled staff with technological sophistication. The introduction of 10-hour rule for running staff and increased leave reserves (30% for running staff, 25% for transportation staff) also contributed to higher staff numbers.

Regarding fuel, the total fuel bill in 1981-82 was about Rs. 690 crores. The report noted that overall losses of coal were 5.7%, exceeding the permissible limit of 2%, attributed to under-loading by collieries and pilferage en route, particularly in Eastern and South-Eastern regions. The quality of coal supplied was found to have deteriorated significantly, with ash content increasing from 15-18% in earlier days to 30-36%. The specific fuel consumption rates for steam traction, both passenger and goods, increased between 1978-79 and 1981-82. Diesel and electric traction were identified as more economical, with steam engines having 8-9% thermal efficiency compared to 25-30% for internal combustion engines. However, the report noted that diesel and steam locomotives were still deployed in electrified sections due to non-electrification of short links. The Comptroller and Auditor General of India's report for 1980-81 indicated excessive diesel consumption and a lack of proper "trip rations" or investigation of excesses on several railways. Furthermore, the installed capacity of the Chittaranjan Locomotive Works for manufacturing diesel and electric locomotives was not fully utilized due to stated "lack of demand" and "lack of funds" despite the need to replace obsolete steam locomotives.

What was recommended

  • The Committee stated that there ought to be an acute awareness of the need for economy in expenses.
  • It should be possible to improve financial performance by augmenting earnings through intensive use of assets and carrying more traffic, without indiscriminate increases in fare and freight rates or leakage of revenue.
  • Concerted and effective steps should be taken to control costs.
  • Particular attention should be paid to the Eastern, North Eastern, Northeast Frontier, and Southern Railways in controlling costs without impairing the efficiency of operations.
  • There is an urgent need for a scientific reappraisal of the Staff Strength employed in various spheres of work, particularly at top levels and in offices where the workload has no direct bearing on the traffic handled.
  • The Committee strongly felt that a critical review of the staffing pattern is urgently called for, covering existing procedures and work conditions, and to ascertain simplifications and streamlining of procedures and improvements in work conditions to effect further economy in expenditure.
  • The Committee noted the recommendation of the Estimates Committee (1982-83) that the strength of non-technical staff in the Production Units of the Railways should be reviewed by an outside agency like the Staff Inspection Unit of the Ministry of Finance, and surplus staff should be retrained and redeployed on new projects productively.
  • A fair and reasonable formula consistent with the need to have promotional avenues for workmen should be evolved early and extended to as many categories as possible without delay. An incentive in achieving this could be graded higher emoluments for men skilled in more than one trade.
  • The process of mechanised maintenance should be speeded up to effect maximum possible economy and consequent reduction in operational cost.
  • A Work Study regarding incentive norms in Production Units should immediately be undertaken, and on the basis of that study, fresh norms of productivity should be determined. The Committee also cited the Estimates Committee's recommendation to update incentive schemes in light of technical sophistication and ensure they operate without malpractice.
  • A critical review of the overall fuel consumption position should be undertaken to evolve better norms, considering technological improvements, and to effect better control on fuel consumption in the interest of economy.
  • The lack of control over fuel consumption, as highlighted by the Comptroller and Auditor General's report, should be remedied early.
  • The Committee noted the Estimates Committee's recommendation (1982-83) that the Ministry of Railways should undertake a cost-benefit analysis of postponing investment on replacing obsolete steam locomotives and keeping the production capacity of diesel locomotives substantially idle, and take up the matter suitably with the Planning Commission.
  • The Committee noted the Estimates Committee's emphasis (1982-83) on the need for replacing obsolete steam locomotives with more efficient Diesel locomotives early.

What it means for citizens

The report indicates that the Indian Railways are facing challenges related to increasing operational costs, driven significantly by staff and fuel expenses. For ordinary citizens, this could imply that the financial health of the railway system is under strain. The committee's call for economy in expenses, efficient staff utilization, and control over fuel consumption suggests efforts to manage costs without solely relying on raising fares and freight rates indiscriminately.

If the recommended measures are implemented successfully—such as a scientific reappraisal of staff strength, faster adoption of mechanised maintenance, updating incentive schemes, and improving fuel efficiency—it could lead to a more financially sound and efficient railway network. This, in turn, may contribute to more reliable services, better maintenance of assets, and potentially mitigate the need for significant increases in passenger fares or freight charges in the future. The emphasis on switching to more economical electric and diesel traction also points towards a more sustainable and modern railway system in the long term, which could indirectly benefit citizens through improved service quality and operational reliability.
