Government's Action on Railway Track Expansion Recommendations

Report date: 1986-04-02

Committee: Railway Convention Committee

Lok Sabha: 08

What was discussed

This Fourth Report of the Railway Convention Committee (1985) details the action taken by the Government on the recommendations from the Committee's Twelfth Report (1980) on the 'Track Expansion Programme of Railways'. The Twelfth Report was presented to Parliament on 25th August, 1984.

The Government's replies to all recommendations were received by 27th September, 1985. The Committee reviewed these replies during sittings on 2nd April, 1986, and adopted this report on the same day.

Out of 25 recommendations in the Twelfth Report, the Committee noted that the Government accepted 10 recommendations (40%). The Committee decided not to pursue 11 recommendations (44%) based on the Government's replies. For 2 recommendations (8%), the Government's replies were not accepted by the Committee, and final replies for another 2 recommendations (8%) were still awaited.

What the report found

The Committee observed that in an inflationary economy, projects (such as new lines and conversion projects) should be completed as quickly as possible. Scarce resources should be utilized for ongoing projects to ensure balanced regional development without discrimination, by prioritizing the commissioning of at least one railway line under construction in each state where a new line was undertaken during the Sixth Five Year Plan.

The Committee noted that the Ministry of Railways repeated a previous reply regarding railway infrastructure dispersal, instead of giving a specific answer to the suggestion that the matter be placed before the Cabinet. The Committee felt this indicated a "scant regard" for its recommendation. It reiterated that the Ministry of Transport should take concerted action to disperse new railway infrastructure across the country to effectively check congestion in metropolitan cities.

The Committee agreed that uneconomic branch lines, especially in areas with developed alternative transport modes, are a burden on the nation. It noted that the Railway Reforms Committee had recommended that 17 uneconomic lines in Gujarat could not be closed because connecting 'Kutcha' roads, which are unserviceable during monsoon, needed to be converted into all-weather metalled roads. The Committee considered that estimating the financial assistance needed for this conversion and offering incentives to the State Government could help achieve the objective of closing these uneconomic branch lines. The Committee also considered that extending dead-end lines (which are at the end of the railway system) to a convenient vital point in the main railway network might make them profitable.

What was recommended

  • The Committee's observation that railway track expansion slowed considerably after 1968-69, with an average addition of 102 kilometers per year from 1969-70 to 1982-83, compared to 331 kilometers per year from 1950-51 to 1968-69, was noted.
  • A suitable system for assessing economic costs and benefits of Railway projects should be developed, and economic cost-benefit analysis should always be performed. The organization of the Economic Adviser of Railways should be qualitatively strengthened for this purpose.
  • Before the actual commencement of construction, survey reports should be updated to account for any new factors. On completion of a survey report, the concerned State Government should be informed and consulted before the proposal is submitted to the Planning Commission for a final decision.
  • While selecting new lines, the Railway Board should strictly follow specified criteria, and offers of contributions (such as free land or earthwork) by a State Government should not be considered at that stage. However, if two projects are equally meritorious after applying strict criteria, preference may be given to one with a State Government contribution to expedite progress, while ensuring that this preference does not neglect backward states.
  • The Railway Board should develop a 20-year long-term perspective plan for track expansion in consultation with the Planning Commission, for implementation from the start of the Seventh Plan. Projects under this plan should be undertaken strictly in order of priority and completed according to their time schedule without interruption.
  • Adequate funds must be allocated for new line construction in each Plan period, and these funds should not be diverted to other Plan heads or specific projects, except in rare cases for optimum resource use.
  • Clear physical targets for new lines, gauge conversion, and doubling should be set, and efforts made to achieve them.
  • Arrears in track renewals should be cleared through a phased program, at least by the end of the next ten years. Additionally, about 5,000 kilometers of track should be added over the next 20 years to meet traffic needs, which means about 1,250 kilometers per Plan period.
  • A separate Metro Railway Authority should be established for each major city to plan, construct, and operate the intra-city railway system.
  • Further concentration of investment in and around the metropolitan cities of Bombay, Calcutta, Madras, and Delhi, and their connecting trunk routes, should be avoided to remove congestion, disperse railway facilities, and develop new growth centers. The Committee reiterated that the Government should give serious consideration to this suggestion and bring it to the notice of both the Cabinet and State Governments. Concerted action should be taken by the Ministry of Transport to ensure new railway infrastructure is suitably dispersed across the country to effectively check metropolitan city congestion.
  • The issue of uneconomic branch lines should be re-examined to determine if they can be made viable by improving speed, changing train timings to suit public convenience, or providing more passenger facilities. The Railway Board should continue dialogue with State Governments for the closure of totally uneconomic lines whose conversion may be viable. Financial assistance and incentives should be estimated and provided to the Gujarat State Government to convert 'Kutcha' roads (unserviceable during monsoon) into all-weather metalled roads for 17 uneconomic lines, to facilitate their withdrawal. The Railway Board should also consider extending dead-end lines to a convenient vital point in the main railway network to potentially make them profitable.

What it means for citizens

Citizens can expect continued efforts to improve the safety and efficiency of existing railway lines through track renewals, with a goal to clear arrears within ten years. There is also a stated commitment to setting clear physical targets for railway expansion and incorporating new lines into a long-term plan, potentially leading to more predictable development of the railway network in the future.

In major cities, the development of Metro Railway Authorities and ongoing projects like the Calcutta Metro (parts of which became operational in October and November 1984) suggest future improvements in urban rail transport services. The Committee's reiterated recommendation to disperse railway infrastructure away from metropolitan centers could lead to better railway connectivity and economic development in currently underserved regions, if acted upon. Discussions between the Railways and State Governments about uneconomic branch lines might result in their closure where adequate road alternatives exist, or in improvements and extensions to make them viable, potentially affecting local travel options and even leading to better all-weather road infrastructure in some areas like Gujarat.
