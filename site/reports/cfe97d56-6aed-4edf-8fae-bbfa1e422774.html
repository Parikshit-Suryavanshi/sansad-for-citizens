Action Taken on Railway Wagon Requirements and Availability, 1971 Recommendations

Report date: 1975-04

Committee: Railway Convention Committee

Lok Sabha: 05

What was discussed

This Seventh Report by the Railway Convention Committee, 1973, details the action taken by the Government on the recommendations from the Fifth Report of the Railway Convention Committee, 1971. The Fifth Report, which focused on "Requirements and Availability of Wagons," was presented to both Houses of Parliament on 1973-04-27. The Government provided their replies on 1974-03-15 and 1974-07-27, indicating the actions taken on the recommendations.

The current Committee's Sub-Committee considered these Government replies at sittings held on 1974-12-16, 1974-12-23, 1974-12-24, 1975-03-12, and 1975-03-18. The report covered several topics, including the forecast of freight traffic, the supply of wagons to trade and industry, coal loading and movement, empty haulage of wagons, and the detention of wagons by Steel Plants. It also analyzed the reasons for persistent shortfalls in freight traffic targets and customer complaints regarding wagon availability.

What the report found

The Committee observed that out of 96 recommendations from the Fifth Report, 60 (62.5%) were accepted by the Government. The Committee did not desire to pursue 12 recommendations (12.5%) based on Government replies, while Government replies for 24 recommendations (25%) were not accepted.

Specific findings include:

  • There was a persistent shortfall in originating freight traffic compared to forecasts during the Third and Fourth Five Year Plan periods. The Ministry of Railways' explanation that "Net Tonne Kilometres" (a measure of freight volume and distance) nearly met targets was found to be "laboured" by the Committee, as the increase in traffic lead was due to fortuitous circumstances not planned for.
  • Despite investments of approximately Rs. 5,336 crores in successive Plans and an additional Rs. 1,686 crores in the Third Plan, actual originating traffic carried at the end of the Third Plan (1965-66) was 203 million tonnes, significantly less than the expected 249 million tonnes. A further investment of Rs. 1,420 crores in the Fourth Plan resulted in carrying only 185 million tonnes in 1973-74, which was less than the 1968-69 figure before the Plan started.
  • The number of wagons available with the Railways progressively increased, reaching 388,026 by the end of 1973-74. However, the originating traffic moved in 1972-73 and 1973-74 was less than in 1969-70, when fewer wagons were available (383,891 wagons moved 207.9 million tonnes in 1969-70, while 388,026 wagons moved 184.9 million tonnes in 1973-74). This led the Committee to conclude that either investment for additional traffic was not properly made or field operations were not managed efficiently.
  • In January 1975, industry and trade representatives expressed continued dissatisfaction with wagon availability. Examples included the aluminium industry receiving not more than 25% of its wagon requirements and the jute industry facing inconvenience in obtaining coal.
  • Outstanding wagon indents on the South Eastern Railway decreased from 85,128 in April 1974 to 36,768 in December 1974, mostly for low-rated traffic. The Eastern Railway had 6,152 outstanding indents for general goods as of 1974-12-31, with some dating back to 1974-09-15 for stone on Howrah Division and 1974-12-21 for general goods on Dhanbad Division.
  • Salt loading on the Rajkot Division of the Western Railway declined from 30,000 wagons in 1968-69 to 19,000 in 1973-74 due to non-availability of wagons, with 33,000 out of 38,000 outstanding indents for salt, some registered in March 1971. The total originating tonnage of salt moved by Railways declined from 2,700,000 tonnes in 1972-73 to 2,339,000 tonnes in 1973-74.
  • While Railway operational efficiency improved after May 1974 (e.g., daily average loading, wagon turn-round time, wagon kilometres per wagon day), the Committee noted that May 1974 was an "abnormal period" due to a railway staff strike threat, and earlier years (e.g., 1961-62) had achieved better performance results in terms of turn-round time and wagon kilometres per wagon day.
  • The Ministry of Railways claimed sufficient covered stock (approximately 52.9% of total wagon holding as on 1974-03-31) to meet requirements, despite complaints of inadequate availability.
  • Constraints on timely wagon availability, according to the Ministry of Railways, included inadequate facilities at transhipment points (97 break-of-gauge points, with 22.3 million tonnes or 12% of total traffic lifted at these points in 1973-74), inadequate capacity of certain sections (74% of freight traffic carried on 25% of total route kilometrage), inadequate facilities at certain terminals (90% of freight traffic generated at about 10% of terminals), and inadequate availability of engines.
  • The Committee was not convinced by the Ministry's contention that inadequate engine availability was a reason for delayed/non-supply of wagons, noting that 536 diesel and 156 electric locos were added during the Fourth Plan while 1204 steam locos were condemned. The Ministry had stated that a shortfall of 21 broad gauge electric and 42 metre gauge diesel locos had "little effect on traffic because the level of traffic actually carried was also lower than anticipation."
  • Coal loading in Bengal-Bihar fields decreased from 5,698 wagons per day in 1972-73 to 5,112 wagons per day in 1973-74, attributed to power cuts, staff agitations, and "go-slows." The actual coal traffic (62.4 million tonnes) was 15.1 million tonnes less than the Fourth Plan target.
  • Empty haulage of wagons increased on some Broad Gauge and Metre Gauge Railways in 1973-74 compared to 1972-73.
  • A joint note from the Ministries of Railways and Steel and Mines on wagon detention at Steel Plants, previously requested, "has not been furnished." A high-level committee set up by the Ministry of Steel and Mines submitted a report on excessive detention to wagons in Steel Plants, which was under examination.
  • Reports from a Study Team on free time for wagon detention in Durgapur, Bhilai, and Rourkela Steel Plants were submitted, and revised instructions issued. However, reports for Tata Iron and Steel Company and Indian Iron and Steel Company were still awaited, and the review of implementation was not yet completed. The Committee "deprecate[d] the inordinate delay of four years" in conducting this examination.
  • 110 diesel locomotives were imported during the Fourth Plan for Steel Plants, largely due to the Ministry of Steel and Mines' inability to assess requirements realistically and on time. The last order for 10 diesel shunters was placed in November 1972, despite an earlier decision not to import more locomotives during the Fourth Plan.
  • The Ministries of Railways and Steel and Mines blamed each other for the "bunching" of raw material trains into Steel Plants, indicating a "failure of coordination machinery."

What was recommended

  • The Committee reiterated the recommendation of the previous Committee that the Government should examine in depth the main reasons for faulty estimates of freight traffic and take effective remedial measures to ensure that the requirements of freight traffic are assessed realistically for the Fifth and subsequent Plan periods.
  • The Committee reiterated its previous recommendations that Railways cannot escape the responsibility for realistic planning, to ensure scarce resources are not locked up in unproductive schemes and full advantage is derived of additional facilities created and assets built up over the years.
  • The Committee observed that mistakes of the past should not recur and Railway Planning during the Fifth Plan should be realistic and strictly related to carefully assessed needs.
  • The Committee stressed that the Eastern and South Eastern Railways should spare no efforts to ensure that all outstanding indents for general and other goods are cleared and the supply of wagons for industrial, commercial and public use is made in time and without delay.
  • The Committee urged the Railways to take effective measures to ensure the clearance of all outstanding indents for wagons for loading of salt and emphasized the imperative need for quick movement of salt meant for remote areas of the country to prevent scarcity.
  • The Committee stressed that the drive to improve the performance of the Railways (since May 1974) should be sustained and intensified so as to surpass the best operational results achieved in the sixties.
  • The Committee stressed that since the number of covered wagons is claimed to be adequate, there should be no difficulty in meeting in full the requirements of public for the covered stock.
  • The Committee hoped that at least during the Fifth Five Year Plan period, concerted measures would be taken to remove constraints (inadequate facilities at a number of transhipment points, inadequate capacity of certain sections, inadequate facilities at certain terminals, and inadequate availability of engines) coming in the way of efficient and speedy movement of wagons.
  • The Committee urged that the Railways, while planning for movement of bulk commodities and higher priority traffic, should not overlook the needs of small scale industries.
  • The Committee suggested that the existing classification of commodities for priority purposes should be made more realistic so as to ensure that the needs of the small scale industries are adequately met.
  • The Committee liked the Railways to take initiative to constitute committees (like the one functioning at Calcutta with industry, trade, and Chief Minister representatives) at other headquarters of the Railways, apart from activising the committee now existing at Calcutta.
  • The Committee stressed that the Railways should render prompter and more satisfactory service to the public.
  • The Committee liked the Government to take concerted action to ensure an in-depth analysis of the deterioration in the handling of coal traffic and to take concrete measures to ensure that mining capacity and rail transport facilities are developed hand in hand to meet user requirements. The Committee would like to be informed of the measures taken and improvements effected.
  • The Committee reiterated the recommendations of the previous Committee that causes of empty haulage should be studied in depth and concrete measures taken to improve the position.
  • The Committee recommended that the Railway Board should indicate the position (Railway-wise) regarding empty haulage of wagons and measures taken for fuller utilisation of rolling stock, particularly of wagons, in their Annual Reports presented to Parliament.
  • The Committee stressed the need for coordination between the Railways and Steel Plant authorities to eliminate the bottlenecks coming in the way of smooth and efficient handling of traffic.
  • The Committee stressed that there should be better coordination between the steel plants and mines and that improvements in siding facilities, signalling, etc., should be effected as necessary, at the earliest.
  • The Committee liked the Ministry to ensure that in future the reports of Study Teams (on detention of wagons in steel plants) are finalised expeditiously.
  • The Committee urged that the review of the implementation of the recommendations of the Study Team (on free time for steel plants) should be expedited and results thereof communicated to them at the earliest.
  • The Committee liked the Government to investigate the matter of importing diesel locomotives for steel plants in all its aspects, with a view to ensure that future requirements are assessed realistically and intimated to the Ministry of Railways well in advance for indigenous manufacture.
  • The Committee liked the Ministries of Railways and Steel and Mines to review the position (regarding bunching of raw material trains) periodically at higher levels so that any difficulty in movement of raw materials to the Steel Plants is resolved without delay.

What it means for citizens

The report highlights ongoing challenges in the Indian Railways' ability to provide wagons in adequate numbers and on time for trade and industry, despite significant past investments. This can lead to delays and increased costs for essential goods like coal and salt, potentially impacting their availability and affordability for ordinary people, including household users and small scale industries.

Citizens may experience continued difficulties in obtaining goods if transport infrastructure constraints, issues with wagon availability, and coordination problems between ministries are not resolved. While there have been some recent improvements in railway operational efficiency, the report stresses the need for sustained efforts to achieve better performance and ensure that the substantial capital investments translate into reliable and complaint-free service for the public. The report calls for better planning, improved coordination, and prompt resolution of issues to ensure that the needs of all users, especially those in remote areas and small industries, are adequately met.
