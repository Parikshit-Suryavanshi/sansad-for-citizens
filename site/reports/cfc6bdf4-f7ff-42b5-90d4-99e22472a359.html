Railway Convention Committee Reviews Suburban Services in Metropolitan Cities

Report date: 1973-02

Committee: Railway Convention Committee

Lok Sabha: 05

What was discussed

This Second Report by the Railway Convention Committee, 1971, focuses on suburban services provided by the Indian Railways, particularly in the metropolitan cities of Bombay, Calcutta, and Madras. The Committee heard evidence from representatives of the Ministry of Railways on 23rd and 24th October, 1972. The report was considered and adopted by the Committee on 25th January, 1973.

The report examined the growth of suburban traffic, the development of services, terminal facilities, the composition and frequency of suburban trains, types of traction power used, classes of accommodation, overcrowding, and the financial aspects of suburban fares, earnings, and losses. It also looked into the historical reasons for designating certain services as "suburban" and the special, lower season ticket fares applicable only in Bombay, Calcutta, and Madras.

The Committee also received memoranda and suggestions from various organizations, Members of Parliament, and individuals regarding the working of Indian Railways. The report includes discussions on the potential extension of suburban services to other metropolitan areas like Delhi, Ahmedabad, Bangalore, Hyderabad, Kanpur, and Poona, as well as the progress of rapid transit systems.

What the report found

The Committee observed significant overcrowding in third class compartments during peak hours, with occupation rates reaching 170-200% in Bombay, 114-179% in Calcutta, and 200-349% in Madras. The growth in suburban traffic from 1950-51 to 1970-71 (292% in Bombay, 333% in Calcutta, 246% in Madras) had far outstripped the increase in the number of trains (167% in Bombay, 220% in Calcutta, 124% in Madras) during the same period up to 1st May, 1972.

Terminal facilities in Bombay, Calcutta, and Madras were found to be inadequate, and Railways only instructed Zonal Railways to conduct necessary studies to optimize existing facilities as late as 18th February, 1971. There was a regrettable delay in undertaking detailed studies of metropolitan transport requirements by the Government until October, 1965, when the Planning Commission constituted a study team. Schemes for Rapid Transit Systems for Bombay, Delhi, and Madras had not yet been finalized, despite more than seven years since the study team was formed, though the Calcutta underground railway was sanctioned on 1st August, 1972.

A considerable number of 1928 vintage Electric Multiple Unit (EMU) rakes were still in use on Central and Western Railways in Bombay. Shortfalls in EMU coach production were noted, partly due to non-agreement on prices with M/s Jessop & Co. and limitations in the supply of electrical equipment from H.E.I.L., Bhopal. Steam traction was still being used for some suburban services in Calcutta and Madras areas because electrification of those sections was deemed not to yield a reasonable return, and sufficient diesel engines were not available. Suburban season ticket fares were lower than non-suburban fares, and while First Class fares were made uniform across the three cities in 1969, Third Class fares were only made uniform up to 48 Kms. on 15th April, 1972, still differing beyond that distance. No scientific cost study of suburban fares had been made by the Railways, though detailed studies were expected to be completed within the next 18 months from the report date. Suburban services in Bombay, Calcutta, and Madras consistently ran at a net deficit, totaling (-) Rs. 11.55 crores in 1970-71.

What was recommended

  • The Railways should present accurate statistics of their "Suburban Services," including only cities/sections where special season ticket fares apply, and explicitly state the meaning of "Suburban Services" in such statistics.
  • Integrated plans for providing suburban services should be prepared expeditiously in consultation with State Governments.
  • The question of managing these services and the authority responsible for them should be decided at the earliest to avoid delays in implementation.
  • Necessary arrangements, including land acquisition at reasonable costs and keeping margins for future growth, should be made in advance and in a planned manner.
  • Time-bound and action-oriented programmes for rail transport development as an integrated part of metropolitan city development plans should be prepared urgently.
  • Pending the introduction of Rapid Transit Systems, the Railways should take immediate measures to improve existing suburban facilities in metropolitan cities.
  • Surveys for improving and augmenting terminal facilities should be expedited, and necessary steps taken to provide these facilities promptly.
  • Where existing terminal facilities are limited by space, alternative terminals should be planned and constructed at suitable locations with top priority.
  • A survey of transport facilities for suburban traffic in all metropolitan cities with a population of 10 lakhs and over should be undertaken, and integrated perspective plans prepared in consultation with relevant authorities.
  • Nine-coach rakes should be introduced on all busy sections in Bombay, Calcutta, and Madras without further delay.
  • Requirements for such coaches in other metropolitan cities should be assessed in advance, and plans for their production and procurement prepared well in time.
  • The Railways should thoroughly investigate the feasibility of introducing 12-coach rakes on busy sections, considering international practices and technological developments.
  • Earnest efforts should be made to expedite the procurement of EMU stock by increasing the manufacturing capacity of the Integral Coach Factory and improving the operations of M/s. Jessop & Co.
  • Government should urgently ensure that electric equipment for EMU coaches, especially for meter gauge coaches, is supplied to the Integral Coach Factory to meet all requirements.
  • The Railways should endeavor to reduce the minimum headway between trains in cities, consistent with safety, and introduce more trains.
  • The Madras Railway Administration, in consultation with the Planning Commission, Ministry of Transport, and State Government, should develop a plan for constructing under/overbridges at important and busy level crossings to increase train frequency.
  • The Ministry of Railways, in consultation with the Ministry of Transport and State Governments, should identify and prepare time-bound programs to provide missing under/overbridges at level crossings that hinder smooth traffic movement.
  • The Railways should study traffic requirements on sections in Calcutta and Madras currently using steam traction to determine the necessary traction and provide it to cope with growing traffic.
  • In the interim, the Railways should prioritize putting diesel engines on these steam-traction sections, particularly during peak hours.
  • Suburban travel should be mass-oriented and provide only one class of travel.
  • While introducing one uniform class, separate compartments for ladies and children under 12 should continue to be earmarked.
  • The Railway Design and Standards Organisation (RDSO) should promptly examine how to optimize accommodation in existing EMU coaches and develop new designs for future coaches, focusing on ventilation, safety, and considering latest technological developments and layouts from other countries.
  • Coaches with new layouts should be tested on a pilot basis before extensive introduction to assess passenger reactions and ensure effectiveness.
  • The remaining diversity in third class suburban season ticket fares beyond 48 Kms. should be removed, and uniform fares prescribed in the three cities.
  • Earnest efforts should be made to complete cost studies of passenger fares expeditiously.
  • The Railways should determine the cost of suburban services separately from other passenger services.
  • After completing the cost study, the Railways may examine adjusting the fare structure, keeping in mind the desirability of providing cheaper travel.
  • The Government should study the extent to which other railway systems in foreign countries provide suburban travel at rates lower than their cost and how any deficits are met.

What it means for citizens

For Indian citizens, especially those in Bombay, Calcutta, and Madras, these recommendations point towards significant improvements in their daily commute. If implemented, they could expect relief from severe overcrowding on suburban trains through more trains, potentially longer trains (12-coach rakes investigated), and improved coach designs with better standing accommodation, ventilation, and safety. There is also a push for greater punctuality and reliability of services.

The report also suggests a move towards a single, uniform class of travel on suburban trains, which could lead to more equitable access for all passengers, though specific provisions for ladies and children under 12 would be maintained. While the report notes that suburban services operate at a loss, it recommends studying the cost structure and potentially adjusting fares while still aiming for affordable travel. Better planning for new and existing railway infrastructure, including terminals and the elimination of level crossings with over/underbridges, aims to reduce traffic congestion and ensure smoother, faster journeys in the long run, not just for rail passengers but also for road users. These efforts are intended to support planned urban development and ease the strain on over-saturated metropolitan areas.
