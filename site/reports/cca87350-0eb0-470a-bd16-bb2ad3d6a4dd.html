Railway Convention Committee Recommends Dividend Rates and Financial Adjustments for 1980-84

Report date: 1982-11-05

Committee: Railway Convention Committee

Lok Sabha: 07

What was discussed

The Railway Convention Committee (1980) reviewed the rate of dividend payable by the Railway Undertaking to the General Revenue, which is like an interest payment, and other related financial matters for the financial years 1980-81 to 1983-84. The Committee was formed on October 1, 1980, to examine how Railway finances interact with General finances.

The committee specifically discussed proposals from the Ministry of Railways, including the continuation of existing dividend concessions for the Sixth Plan Period (1980-85). Key topics covered were the rates at which Railways should pay dividends, payments to State Governments in lieu of passenger fare tax, contributions to the Depreciation Reserve Fund (used for renewing railway assets), and the Development Fund (used for passenger amenities and operational efficiency improvements). The Committee gathered information from representatives of the Ministries of Railways and Finance on October 22, 1982, and finalized this report on November 2, 1982.

What the report found

The report observed that the average rate of dividend paid by the Railways has been consistently lower than the average borrowing rate of the Government of India since 1977-78. When excluding the 1% dividend component allocated for State Governments in place of passenger fare tax, the Railways' contribution to General Revenues has been a negative figure since 1972-73.

As of March 31, 1981, the Railways owed General Revenues approximately Rs. 603.54 crores, which included deferred dividend liability (unpaid interest) and loans to the Development Fund. This indebtedness was projected to be around Rs. 600.94 crores by the end of 1981-82 and Rs. 536.99 crores in the 1982-83 budget estimates. The Ministry of Finance agreed to the proposed differential dividend rates, acknowledging that this would result in a reduction of revenue to the General Budget, given the Railways' financial state. The committee noted that these proposals would lead to a shortfall of about Rs. 300 crores in covering bare interest charges on the total capital invested in Railways (Rs. 6096 crores as of March 31, 1981) during the 1980-85 period.

The report also found a "serious situation" due to a large backlog in renewing and replacing Railway assets, making rehabilitation the main focus of the Sixth Plan. The Railway Reforms Committee estimated a need for an additional Rs. 309 crores (net) for the Depreciation Reserve Fund in 1982-83, though the Ministry of Railways indicated it could only increase the contribution by Rs. 56 crores for that year.

What was recommended

  • For the period 1980-84, a dividend rate of 6% should be adopted for capital invested up to March 31, 1980. This rate includes 1.5% on capital invested up to March 31, 1964, for payments to States in lieu of passenger fare tax.
  • A mean percentage of 6.5% should be adopted for dividend payments on capital invested in the Railways after March 31, 1980.
  • Payments to States in lieu of passenger fare tax should be calculated by computing dividend at 1.5% (instead of the existing 1%) of the capital up to March 31, 1964, less any subsidy. From this, Rs. 23.12 crores should be paid to the States, and the remaining amount should be used to assist States in financing safety works. Further increases should be considered based on the recommendations of the Eighth Finance Commission.
  • Existing dividend reliefs and other equitable concessions for calculating dividend payable by the Railways to General Revenues should continue.
  • The entire capital on the Sambalpur-Titlagarh ore line should be exempt from dividend payment, subject to usual conditions.
  • Balances in the various Railway Reserve Funds (excluding the Development Fund) should carry the same interest rate as the dividend actually paid.
  • Any shortfall in dividend payment, to the extent of the interest at the average borrowing rate on the entire capital plus a token contribution, should be shown as an additional subsidy in the estimates presented to Parliament. This could currently be a grant for renewals and replacement of Railway assets.
  • The Railways should substantially increase their earning capacity by applying stricter tests of remunerativeness to commercial investment proposals, consistent with the rising cost of capital.
  • The appropriation to the Depreciation Reserve Fund should be increased from Rs. 500 crores to Rs. 556 crores in 1982-83.
  • For the financial year 1983-84, the contribution to the Depreciation Reserve Fund should be suitably increased, considering the assessment of the Railway Reforms Committee and the Railways' ability to raise additional resources.
  • The current arrangement for obtaining loans from General Revenues to cover Development Fund expenditure, if surpluses are insufficient or non-existent, should continue. The interest on these loans should be charged at the rate applicable to loans provided to State Governments.

What it means for citizens

This report outlines how the Indian Railways manages its finances, including the "dividend" it pays to the central government for capital invested, which is like an interest payment. The committee found that the Railways has not been generating enough money to fully cover these payments and has built up unpaid interest (deferred dividend liability) and loans from the government.

To help the Railways manage its financial challenges, the committee recommended allowing it to pay lower dividend rates than the government's average cost of borrowing. The report also addresses the insufficient funds for essential maintenance and upgrades of railway assets. The committee approved an increase in the money set aside for repairing and replacing old tracks, coaches, and wagons (from the Depreciation Reserve Fund), which is intended to help modernize infrastructure and potentially lead to safer and more reliable train services. Furthermore, the report notes that Railways incur losses by providing public services like suburban trains and certain less profitable routes, which impacts its overall earnings. The payments made to State Governments, which compensate them for a passenger fare tax, were also discussed, with a recommendation for an increased amount to be passed on to the states for safety works and other public welfare initiatives.
