Railway Convention Committee Reports on Ongoing Line Projects

Report date: 1987-12-07

Committee: Railway Convention Committee

Lok Sabha: 08

What was discussed

This Ninth Report by the Railway Convention Committee (1985) examined "On-Going Railway Line Projects." The Indian Railways, described as Asia's largest and the world's second largest State-owned railway system under single management, had a route kilometrage of 61,836 kms as of March 31, 1986. During the year 1985-86, 109 kms of new rail lines were opened to traffic.

The Committee took evidence from representatives of the Ministry of Railways (Railway Board) on August 5 and September 1, 1986, and from the Planning Commission on February 10, 1987. The Committee specifically selected 20 out of 41 new line projects that were in progress as of July 31, 1985, for detailed examination. These projects included lines in the Northern, North-Eastern, South Central, South Eastern, and Western Railways. The report also discussed policy regarding funding of these projects during the Seventh Plan period, including lines in the North Eastern Region, project-oriented lines, and lines where foundation stones had been laid by high dignitaries.

What the report found

The report found that the total plan allocation for new lines in the Seventh Five Year Plan (1985-90) was Rs. 350 crores, which constituted 2.8% of the total plan allocation for Railways (Rs. 12,334 crores). This was the lowest percentage in all seven plans. The Secretary of the Planning Commission acknowledged that the Rs. 350 crores allotted for new lines was inadequate and did not account for inflation.

At the start of the Sixth Five Year Plan (1980-81), 29 new lines were under construction, requiring Rs. 240 crores for completion. Although Rs. 380 crores were initially allotted for these and 22 new lines, the provision was reduced to Rs. 314 crores due to financial constraints, with actual expenditure being Rs. 325 crores. Only 14 new lines were opened, and 10 of these were only partially completed. At the commencement of the Seventh Plan (1985-86), construction works on 48 new lines were in progress, requiring Rs. 1320 crores for completion, with 15 sanctioned between 1969-70 and 1978-79. The Committee observed that the situation at the start of the Seventh Plan was not better than in 1980-81, and delays lead to time and cost overruns.

The anticipated cost for the 20 projects selected for examination was Rs. 613.56 crores at the time of approval, but the latest estimated cost increased to Rs. 1600.09 crores, representing an increase of nearly two and a half times. Expenditure and outlay for these projects up to September 1985 and for 1985-86 were deemed proportionately meagre. Out of the 20 selected lines, only Apta-Roha had been completed by March 1986. Eight lines (Dharmanagar-Kumarghat, Silchar-Jiribam, Lalabazar-Bhairabi, Ernakulam-Alleppey, Bibinagar-Nadikude, Kota-Chittaurgarh-Neemuch, Bhuj-Naliya, and Apta-Roha) were planned for completion during the Seventh Plan. The total requirement for completing all ongoing projects was stated to be Rs. 1500 crores.

The report also noted instances where project-oriented lines, such as Manickgarh to Chandur, did not prove remunerative because anticipated traffic from proposed cement plants did not materialize. The Gauhati-Burnihat line project was deleted from the Railways' Works Programme because the Meghalaya Government did not finalize the terminal station. The Amguri-Tuli line's progress was affected by a territorial dispute between Assam and Nagaland over land ownership. The Planning Commission, Ministry of Finance, and Ministry of Railways held differing views on the funding of project-oriented lines. The Planning Commission and Finance Ministry have advised Railways not to accept financial contributions from State Governments or the private sector for new lines, unless the full cost is covered by the user department or organization.

What was recommended

  • The Railways should pay greater attention to completing on-going railway line projects that have been pending for a long time and should not allow them to linger from one Plan to another.
  • Special care should be taken to allocate more funds to projects in the North Eastern Region.
  • The Ministry of Railways (Railway Board) should examine the possibility of taking up one railway line after another in the North Eastern Region instead of all six projects simultaneously, after consulting the Northeast Council, given resource constraints and difficult terrain.
  • Existing on-going railway line projects should be completed on a priority basis.
  • Target dates, even if tentative, should be fixed first for parts of projects and then for projects as a whole, with funds allocated accordingly.
  • A high priority should be given to on-going railway line projects in states where no new railway line has been constructed or completed since Independence, such as the Nangal Dam-Talwara railway line in Himachal Pradesh.
  • In states with multiple lines under construction, one line should be selected for speedy construction and early completion on a priority basis.
  • Project-oriented lines should be treated as part of the project itself, and their expenditure should be part of the total project expenditure.
  • The accounting system for taking over or handing over assets after a certain period should be worked out.
  • There should be a commitment from the user Ministry or State in cases of project-oriented lines.
  • If projects do not share the cost of construction for their dedicated lines, the Railways should shift emphasis from project-oriented lines to on-going railway line projects meant for the development of backward areas.

What it means for citizens

The report indicates that the slow pace of railway line construction, due to inadequate funding and rising costs, means that citizens will have to wait longer for new or improved rail transport services. Delays in completing projects result in higher costs, which means more public money is spent than originally planned. This also delays the economic and social benefits that these new lines are intended to bring, particularly for backward regions and areas requiring essential transport capacity. For project-oriented lines, if industries or projects do not materialize as expected, the public investment in those railway lines might not be fully utilized, leading to underperforming infrastructure. The policy disagreements on funding project-oriented lines and reluctance to accept external contributions suggest that railway expansion will continue to be constrained by central government allocations, potentially slowing down development in areas needing rail connectivity.
