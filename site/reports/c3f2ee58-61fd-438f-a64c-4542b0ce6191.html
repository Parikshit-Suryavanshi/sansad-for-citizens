Government Reviews Action on Recommendations for Decongesting Railway Routes

Report date: 2002-12-19

Committee: Railway Convention Committee

Lok Sabha: 13

What was discussed

This Sixth Report of the Railway Convention Committee (1999) details the action taken by the Government on the recommendations and observations contained in the Committee's Fourth Report. The Fourth Report focused on the ‘Development of Alternative Routes for Decongesting Existing Routes’.

The Fourth Report was presented to the Lok Sabha on 26 February, 2002, and laid in the Rajya Sabha on 13 March, 2002. It contained 20 observations and recommendations. The Ministry of Railways (Railway Board) provided action taken replies for all these points. The Committee reviewed these replies and adopted the current Draft Report at a sitting held on 26 November, 2002.

What the report found

The Committee noted that a railway line is declared saturated when it exceeds its chartered capacity, which considers factors such as train speeds, gradients, signaling standards, distances between crossing stations, and daily maintenance blocks. A total line section is deemed saturated at 90% utilization of its chartered capacity (after accounting for a mandatory 4-hour maintenance block), while a single line section is considered saturated at over 80% utilization.

The report found that the Golden Quadrilateral, which connects Mumbai, Delhi, Kolkata, and Chennai, along with its two diagonal routes, constitutes 15.8% of the total network but carries over 50% of all freight transport and 47% of passenger traffic. This high-density network is currently "totally saturated" and requires substantial investment in its basic infrastructure, especially as future growth is projected along these corridors. Other sections also experience saturation in line capacity utilization.

The Committee observed that the Railways have faced an "acute financial crunch" for many years, struggling to generate sufficient surpluses. It noted a "huge shelf of projects" totaling about Rs. 35,000 crore, while the Railways' fund balances were very low. The Ministry of Railways had deferred dividend payments to the General Exchequer by Rs. 1500 crore in 2000-01 and Rs. 1000 crore in 2001-02. New projects were often initiated without completing existing ones, leading to thinly spread funds and delayed realization of benefits or revenue. Project priorities frequently changed with changes in government or the Railway Minister.

The Committee also noted that gauge conversion projects under "Unigauge" (converting Meter Gauge and Narrow Gauge lines) were undertaken without assessing their financial viability or operational priority. For instance, the Gondia-Chandafort-Ballarshah line (252 Kms.) in the Nagpur Division of South Eastern Railway was converted at significant cost but remains underutilized, handling only two pairs of passenger trains per day despite a potential for six trains each way. Electrification work was also found to be left incomplete in many places, resulting in blocked investments. The Ministry of Railways incurred higher electricity tariffs for electric traction, sometimes three to four times the cost of generation, even though it is a major consumer and pays on time. As of March 2000, State Electricity Boards (SEBs) owed the Railways Rs. 1,321.81 crore, with Badarpur Thermal Power Station (BTPS) alone accounting for 72% of these outstanding dues and not complying with prepayment. The total outstanding dues from SEBs increased from Rs. 1061.28 crore on 31 December, 1996, to Rs. 1616.45 crore by March 2002.

Heavy congestion at terminals also existed, causing long waiting periods for trains. The Committee found that high horsepower electric locomotives provide benefits such as higher acceleration, the ability to haul heavier loads at higher speeds, and increased reliability, with regenerative braking features saving energy and reducing overall running time. Furthermore, some double-line sections exhibited line capacity utilization as high as 142% after accounting for maintenance blocks, raising concerns about potential compromises to safety. New trains were introduced, and existing train frequencies were increased on routes that had already reached their chartered capacity. The report also highlighted that railway transport is 5 to 6 times more energy-efficient, cost-effective, and eco-friendly compared to road transport, yet the railway's share in goods and passenger traffic declined significantly from 1950-51 to 1996-97 (goods traffic from 89% to 40%, passenger traffic from 80% to 20%). A committee was formed to assess the relative benefits of electrification versus diesel traction.

What was recommended

  • The Ministry of Railways should formulate a well-defined plan for decongesting existing saturated or nearly saturated routes by creating additional capacity through gauge conversion, doubling or laying multiple lines, new alignments, electrification, upgrading signaling and interlocking, splitting long block sections, automatic signaling, introducing air brakes, increasing average speed, creating speed differentials, procuring higher capacity wagons/coaches, adding more wagons/coaches per train, procuring high horsepower locomotives, and developing alternative railway terminals/station facilities. Timely steps should be taken for nearly saturated routes to create additional capacity in advance.
  • The Ministry of Railways should make concrete efforts to increase the generation of internal resources and complete projects nearing completion by investing sufficient funds.
  • Existing projects should be critically reviewed and prioritized after a realistic assessment of their state of completion, financial viability, and for optimum utilization of scarce funds. Priorities, once fixed, should not be changed except for the larger national interest or strategic considerations.
  • The Railways should make proper utilization of the Gondia-Ballarshah line by introducing more goods trains or diverting traffic for South-bound trains.
  • Capacity utilization of similar other lines should also be enhanced.
  • Non-viable projects that are socially desirable should only be taken up when matching funds are assured by both the Planning Commission and the Ministry of Finance.
  • The prevalent practice of sanctioning projects liberally without ensuring the availability of requisite funds for their completion and commissioning must be curbed.
  • Fiscal and other mechanisms should be developed to stimulate the flow of private capital into the rail sector without generating unbearable market interest rates and an unmanageable debt trap.
  • Railways should make serious efforts to involve the private sector in the creation and maintenance of railway networks and assets, potentially by starting some pilot projects on certain routes on an experimental basis.
  • The gaps left on various projects relating to electrification of tracks should be completed at the earliest.
  • For payment of electricity charges, Railways should be treated on par with bulk consumers and charged at a reasonable rate, depending on the cost of electricity production in a particular State.
  • The Ministry of Railways should approach the Cabinet to impose higher rates of cut from the Central Plan Assistance to State Electricity Boards (SEBs) that are heavily defaulting on dues.
  • The amount recoverable from the State Electricity Boards, etc., should be adjusted against their future power tariff bills.
  • The Ministry of Railways should continue and ensure strict adherence to the "cash and carry" scheme for all consignments to be booked in the future to curb fresh accruals to freight outstandings.
  • A blueprint of the specific steps taken by the Ministry of Railways to handle the expected increase in traffic by upgrading existing coaching and freight terminal facilities and providing new terminals should be brought out and placed before the Committee within six months.
  • The main thrust should be to have more platforms, more stabling lines, and more maintenance pits, and these facilities should be developed consistent with the introduction of new originating trains from the terminals.
  • With technology transfer from ABB, Chittaranjan Locomotive Works (CLW) should start manufacturing high horsepower electric locomotives domestically for deployment in the Railways on a larger scale.
  • The work of gauge conversion in missing links such as Jayanagar-Darbhanga-Narkatiyaganj and Darbhanga to Samastipur, Barauni and Patna, including a 3 Kms stretch in between, as well as lines like Gonda-Gorakhpur loop and a line between New Jalpaiguri to New Bongaigaon, should be taken up and completed at the earliest to ensure the entire line starts functioning.
  • The Ministry of Railways should utilize the line capacity only to the extent that it does not affect basic safety norms and affect human life and/or loss of public property.
  • Compliance to safety norms by the Railways should be given priority while lines are optimally utilized.
  • A temporary ban should be imposed on fresh projects, including the expansion of existing projects in the name of material modification.
  • The Railways should concentrate on introducing advanced signaling networks, reducing the speed differential between different trains, and increasing traffic on the line to increase the line capacity of the existing network.
  • Modern technology should be introduced so that the exact location of every train is known, and centralized traffic control is promoted to manage trains with different speeds according to their priority.
  • Faster turnaround time for rolling stock on trunk routes should be ensured to utilize them better for more loading, leading to more revenues for Railways and reducing pressure on the system.
  • The concept of ‘Shadow goods trains’ should be utilized on most of the congested routes to reduce the travel time of goods/passenger trains.
  • The Ministry of Railways must conduct a detailed exercise to assess the line capacity before introducing new train services or increasing the frequency of existing trains on saturated routes.
  • The matter of considering non-viable but socially desirable projects as part of an integrated development plan for the transport sector should be referred to the Ministry of Finance and the Ministry of Surface Transport for their consideration.
  • The Ministry of Railways should also persuade the Ministry of Finance and the Ministry of Surface Transport for an integrated development plan so that road and railway sectors may supplement each other in the development of an area.

What it means for citizens

The report's focus on decongesting existing railway routes and improving railway infrastructure has several practical implications for ordinary citizens. If the recommendations are implemented, it could lead to more efficient and reliable train services, potentially reducing travel times and delays for both passenger and freight trains. The push for completing pending projects, enhancing line capacity through measures like doubling lines and better signaling, and utilizing existing routes more effectively, could result in more train services and better connectivity across the country.

Efforts to improve terminal facilities, such as more platforms and maintenance pits, could reduce waiting times at stations and improve the overall passenger experience. The emphasis on safety norms, even on highly utilized routes, is crucial for protecting the lives and property of passengers and the public. Improved financial management of railway projects and the recovery of outstanding dues from electricity boards could contribute to the Railways' financial health, potentially allowing for more investment in services without necessarily increasing fares. The development of high-horsepower electric locomotives could lead to faster and more efficient train operations. An integrated transport plan, combining road and rail development, could mean better overall accessibility and development in various regions, benefiting citizens through improved movement of goods and people.
